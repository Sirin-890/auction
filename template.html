Part 1 · Second-price sealed-bid auction

Bidder 1 (value v ∈ {0.2, 0.5, 0.8}) bids b from 0.00 to 1.00. Each rival draws a value ~ U[0,1] and bids truthfully, half its value, or randomly. Curves show Bidder 1's average utility; the dashed pink line marks the truthful bid b = v, the green dot the best bid on the grid.

Do these plots help Bidder 1 choose a strategy? Yes — but only to confirm that bidding truthfully is optimal. In all nine panels, the peak sits at (or within Monte-Carlo noise of) b = v, no matter which strategy the rival uses. The rival's strategy changes the height of the curve, not the location of its peak. So Bidder 1 does not need to know anything about the rival: truth-telling is a dominant strategy.

Part 1B · Truthful bidding against fixed rival bids

Fix the rival's bid at 0.1, 0.4 or 0.9 and sweep Bidder 1's bid. Utility = v − rival bid if you win, else 0.

Bidder 1 value:
In all 9 cases (and identically with two rivals, since only the highest rival bid sets the price) b = v is among the utility-maximising bids. Truthful bidding is never strictly worse — it wins exactly when winning is profitable.

Part 2 · First-price auction with n bidders

Bidder 1 bids k·v (k from 0.1 to 2.0); the other n−1 bidders bid the equilibrium (n−1)/n · v. Values ~ U[0,1]. The dashed line is the equilibrium multiplier.

The utility peaks at the grid point nearest the equilibrium (n−1)/n — deviating up (overbidding) loses margin, deviating down loses wins. “Could the winner have paid less?” is essentially 100% of the time in first-price auctions (the winner always bids above the runner-up); the informative number is the mean overpayment, which matches the theory value (n−1)/(n(n+1)).
